Abstract:  To fully grasp the corporate information disclosure cycle, one needs to study both greenwashing and 
brownwashing (the shrouding of negative information).
While greenwashing research has grown substantially, strategic underreporting (brownwashing) has received 
less attention.
This study investigates the combined effect of voluntary sustainability-standard adoption and internal 
governance on brownwashing by publicly listed firms in the five largest ASEAN economies (ASEAN-5) from 2014 
to 2024.
Using panel models with firm- and year-fixed effects on 1,506 firm-year observations from 385 listed companies, 
we examine brownwashing across both the extensive margin (decision to report) and intensive margin (degree 
of underreporting).
The adoption of the Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) framework decreases the likelihood of brownwashing.
However, once brownwashing occurs, estimates indicate a 13.37% increase in underreporting severity.
This extensive-margin effect is more precisely estimated than the intensive-margin effect.
Across robustness checks, the directional pattern holds, though statistical significance is weaker in the smallest 
subsamples.
No direct evidence connects board gender diversity or board sustainability committees to brownwashing, notably 
in an environment where female directors occupy an average of only 18.97% of seats.
Sustainability committees also show no association with GRI adoption, indicating that external reporting 
guidelines and internal governance structures act as independent channels.
These findings broaden the scope of ESG decoupling research and suggest that regulators in developing 
economies should pair voluntary reporting standards with independent assurance rather than relying solely on 
board governance.
Keywords: ASEAN-5; Board gender diversity; Brownwashing; ESG decoupling; Global Reporting Initiative (GRI); 
Sustainability committee.
Contribution/Originality:  This  study investigates whether the adoption of GRI frameworks impacts the level 
of corporate brownwashing in two margins: extensive and intensive, and whether external GRI and internal 
governance mechanisms act as substitutes or complements.
Findings show no significant relationship.
Sustainability committee presence is not a significant determinant of GRI adoption.
Neither sustainability committees nor board gender diversity are significant determinants of corporate 
brownwashing.
The main implication is that these governance mechanisms operate through distinct channels.
As societal and regulatory pressures increase, firms rely more on non-financial disclosures to acquire legitimacy 
(Eliwa et al., 2023).
Effective sustainability practices can yield direct market benefits including reduced cost of capital and increased 
firm value.
However, the challenge is that sustainability reporting is voluntary and not directly verifiable.
This creates information asymmetry between corporate insiders and external parties (Roszkowska-Menkes et al., 
2024), leaving room for strategic disclosure where there is a gap between firms' words and their actual practices.
We call this misalignment between sustainability reporting and real firm behavior ESG (environmental, social and 
governance) information decoupling.
It can take two forms: firms exaggerating their environmental ethics (greenwashing) or firms minimizing their 
sustainability achievements (brownwashing).
Initial perspectives treated decoupling as unidirectional, mainly focusing on low sustainability firms overstating 
their ethical commitment.
But Kim and Lyon (2015) challenged this narrow view: decoupling is bidirectional, with firms brownwashing by 
understating genuine sustainability gains they have achieved.
Firms employ these strategies to shape stakeholder expectations and avoid the operational and reputational costs 
of setting performance goals they may fail to meet.
The literature on decoupling has three distinct gaps.
First, the thematic gap: current research focuses heavily on greenwashing with less attention to brownwashing.
Second, the structural gap: research on external drivers like the adoption of Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) 
standards and internal drivers like having diverse boards or sustainability committees too often occurs in silos, 
with each driver viewed as one of many in a system of independent effects.
Third, the geographic gap: empirical evidence on decoupling remains concentrated in Western and developed 
markets, leaving limited insight into fast-growing ASEAN-5 (the five largest economies of the Association of 
Southeast Asian Nations), which are attracting ESG investments in massive quantities.
The heterogeneity of their disclosure frameworks is stark: Singapore (Singapore Exchange, SGX) has mandated 
ESG disclosure earlier than its regional peers, while others still have voluntary or transitional regimes.
This paper examines the roles of reporting standards and internal governance mechanisms in ASEAN-5 
economies (Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, Indonesia, and the Philippines) on corporate brownwashing from 2014 
to 2024.
ASEAN-5 is an interesting context for studying the drivers of bidirectional decoupling given the diversity in 
regulatory governance and the scale of the economies.
Drawing on four theories, namely neoinstitutional, stakeholder-agency, gender socialization, and resource 
dependence theory, we formulate and test four hypotheses.
First, GRI adoption reduces corporate brownwashing (H1).
Second, greater board gender diversity lowers the extent of corporate underreporting (H2).
Third, existence of sustainability committee (H3) directly lowers brownwashing.
Fourth, sustainability committee existence increases the likelihood of GRI adoption (H4).
Results from the fixed-effects panel analyses reveal a narrower pattern of relationships than hypothesized.
Adoption of the GRI reduces brownwashing on both the extensive and intensive margins, with a more precisely 
estimated effect at the extensive margin.
However, corporate board gender diversity and the existence of sustainability committees show no significant 
relationship with brownwashing.
The paper makes three related contributions to the literature on disclosure.
First, from a conceptual point of view, it contributes to the literature by refining the notion of greenwashing 
through decomposing information decoupling into positive and negative exclusive parts and studying the latter 
in isolation.
Second, it studies the interaction between external disclosure standardization and internal governance 
mechanisms.
The evidence suggests the absence of a reinforcing effect: the presence of sustainability committees does not 
facilitate the adoption of GRI either as a substitute or a catalyst.
Third, it distinguishes the extensive from the intensive margin of underreporting, showing that standardized 
frameworks reduce both the likelihood that brownwashing starts and its severity once underway, with effects 
similar in magnitude but more precisely estimated for prevention than for cure.

Strong ESG disclosure leads to increased legitimacy and better access to capital, reflected in lower cost of debt 
and higher valuations (Eliwa et al., 2023; Saeed et al., 2025).
This presents a tangible incentive for companies to disclose strong sustainability performance.
However, sustainability reporting is mostly voluntary and hard to verify (Roszkowska-Menkes et al., 2024).
This leads to a situation where stated practices may diverge from actual conduct.
Some companies portray inflated image of ethical conduct that does not match their practices (Cepêda, 2026; 
MacLean & Behnam, 2010).
In the short term this may boost the reputation but once uncovered it results in market penalties (Di & Li, 2023; 
Eliwa et al., 2023).
The concept of decoupling stems from the neoinstitutional theory where organizations adopt formal policies to 
symbolically comply without changing their actual practices (Meyer & Rowan, 1977; Roszkowska-Menkes et al., 
2024).
Kim and Lyon (2015) expanded this into a bidirectional framework where organizations seeking to avoid 
scrutiny may either overstate their positive actions (greenwashing) or understate their genuine achievements 
(brownwashing).
Recent literature begins to operationalize the conceptualization of a reporting-performance gap in ESG practices.
Hawn and Ioannou (2016) pioneered empirics by contrasting nominal ESG scores with controversy-adjusted 
scores.
Building on that work, an emerging stream of research conceptualizes decoupling as bidirectional rather than a 
unidirectional construct (Moreno-Ureba, 2026; Yue et al., 2026).
Prior studies identify external and internal forces that limit decoupling.
Externally, standardized reporting frameworks and assurance serve as limits to symbolic ESG practices 
(García-Sánchez et al., 2022).
Yue et al.
(2026) identify an internal limit: the positive feedback of ESG performance can increase stakeholders' 
expectations of CSR such that brownwashing is a means to alleviate scrutiny.
Eliwa et al.
(2023) show that female board representation correlates with a lower degree of ESG decoupling, and the 
presence of sustainability committees (Qiu et al., 2026) and specialized board expertise (Moreno-Ureba, 2026) 
shape ESG reporting.
Conceptualizations of bidirectional decoupling remain vague in our field, with most concentrating on 
greenwashing by itself (Moreno-Ureba, 2026).
Likewise, there are only a few explorations of the joint effect of external forces such as GRI adoption and internal 
governance mechanisms.
Moreno-Ureba (2026) calls for research that disaggregates decoupling and tests the moderating role of internal 
governance; this study takes up that call by analyzing GRI adoption and internal governance together.
Global standardization of sustainability reporting provides a key external guardrail to opportunistic disclosure.
Standardized non-financial metrics built around structured and uniform reporting frameworks enable 
cross-corporate comparability (Friske, 2024; Sannino et al., 2020).
Over the last two decades, standards have gradually incorporated more multi-dimensional indicators into their 
templates, resulting in an increase in disclosure quality and a decrease in corporate opaqueness (García-Sánchez 
et al., 2022; Roszkowska-Menkes et al., 2024).

There are two competing views on how uniform reporting influences strategic disclosure: as a genuine 
information-discipline mechanism, or as an opportunity for narrative camouflage, developed formally in Section 
3.2.
Our evidence indicates that in the ASEAN-5 countries the key voluntary reporting framework is the Global 
Reporting Initiative (GRI), adopted by 85.16% of firms.
The aggregate figure masks cross-country heterogeneity in the pace of adoption and quality of compliance with 
standards.
Institutional paths diverged, with Singapore pushing mandatory general ESG disclosure early on, though the 
choice to report specifically under GRI remains voluntary even there, as Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and 
Philippine firms staggered through multiple phases of voluntary guidance and new frameworks.
This heterogeneity manifests in an uneven spread of standardized reporting during 2014-2024, with adoption 
driven by institutional pressure in home countries (García-Sánchez et al., 2022; Sannino et al., 2020).
It was founded to harmonize reporting of non-financial information, but in the context of ASEAN-5, with uneven 
enforcement and institutional differences, firms will still diverge.
Narrative camouflage will persist in weak-oversight regions via selective disclosure to highlight positive aspects 
and to obscure negative ones.
Stakeholder theory is the underpinning theory since stakeholders are "any group or individual who can affect or 
is affected by the achievement of the organization's objectives" (Freeman, 1984, p. 46), and the stakeholder 
groups provide resources a firm needs to survive and grow and demand accountability (Roszkowska-Menkes et 
al., 2024).
Note that stakeholders hold misaligned interests.
For example, community groups may seek more environmental disclosure whereas shareholders may worry that 
such disclosure jeopardizes short-term profits.
Executives, as agents of the firm, must manage these competing demands (Qiu et al., 2026).
Stakeholder-agency theory helps explain this behavior by arguing that differences in the sources of power and 
information asymmetry influence which demands managers respond to, and the internal governance structures 
constrain opportunistic disclosure that aligns manager behavior with stakeholder expectations (Moreno-Ureba, 
2026).
Information discipline, the pressure that standardized, comparable metrics place on managers to align disclosed 
and actual practice, should improve transparency when firms adopt frameworks such as the GRI, which equips 
firms with a vocabulary for rigorous and comparable disclosure (Friske, 2024; Wang et al., 2026).
Information discipline is the mechanism through which GRI adoption should reduce brownwashing.
Standardized data collection and reporting across multiple ESG indicators makes firm performance easier to 
evaluate by rating agencies and analysts and so increases the visibility cost of what Kim and Lyon (2015) term 
“green hushing,” the informal label for the same brownwashing behavior this study measures.
This in turn should create pressure on managers to narrow the negative decoupling gap by aligning actual conduct 
with disclosed commitments.
However, as mentioned before, a less optimistic view is narrative camouflage.
Since voluntary guidelines such as GRI allow flexible application symbolic compliance remains possible 
(Roszkowska-Menkes et al., 2024).
Because sustainability reporting is voluntary and unverifiable, insiders know more about actual practices than 
outside stakeholders can observe, and managers might use standardized metrics as a “ceremonial shield” to 
draw attention to favorable data points and obscure unfavorable ones in lengthy qualitative disclosures (Boiral et 
al., 2019; Wijen, 2014).
From this perspective voluntary standards may not function as a binding constraint on communication and could 
even enable misrepresentation rather than transparency (Roszkowska-Menkes et al., 2024).
Section 5 tests which of these two effects, information discipline or narrative camouflage, dominates empirically.
Hypothesis 1 (H1).
GRI reporting-standard adoption correlates negatively with corporate brownwashing.
The literature widely accepts the link between board gender diversity and firm transparency (Eliwa et al., 2023; 
Moreno-Ureba, 2026).
Gender socialization and stakeholder-agency theories provide the major foundation for this link.
These theories suggest that male and female directors have distinct cognitive and ethical frameworks and that 
they vary in their tolerance of risk because of their different socialization (Boulouta, 2013; Manita et al., 2018).
This division has ramifications for a board's oversight capacity in practice.
Research indicates that female directors have a greater orientation toward stakeholders and a greater sensitivity 
to social and environmental concerns (Eagly et al., 2003; Eliwa et al., 2023).
Gender diverse boards, then, should be more vigilant monitors, less likely to accept unethical or deceptive firm 
disclosure (Manita et al., 2018) and less likely to excuse managers for misreporting.
The opposing perspective, though, is a more guarded one.
Adding women to the board may not produce effective governance, since the dynamics of inequality and 
tokenism can diminish their influence (Kanter, 1977; Konrad et al., 2008; Manita et al., 2018).
Board composition alone is not sufficient; the level of influence women hold within the board matters as much.
Healthy male majority on board may dilute board's ethical voice because prevailing norms absorb or suppress 
that voice (Torchia et al., 2011).
Again, female directors may not have enough tenure or authority to influence decisions (Moreno-Ureba, 2026).
Practically, extending disclosure requires less organizational will than changing core practices.
Thus, female directors might affect reporting practices more than core practices, decreasing brownwashing when 
performance is strong but increasing greenwashing when performance is weak.
Even so, female directors' monitoring role should still deter non-disclosure overall, supporting a negative sign for 
H2.
Hypothesis 2 (H2) follows: board gender diversity correlates negatively with corporate brownwashing.
It is important to study external voluntary standards and internal corporate governance jointly when explaining 
strategic disclosure (Qiu et al., 2026).
Resource dependence theory and stakeholder-agency theory suggest internal governance, especially 
sustainability committees, works with GRI adoption as a complementary control.
Board-level sustainability committees should improve the oversight of ESG risks and reporting.
This is relevant for brownwashing and reporting as well.
First, a board-level sustainability committee is a subgroup of the board of directors dedicated to handling ESG 
risks and reporting (Qiu et al., 2026).
Structurally, sustainability committees provide the board of directors with additional capacity and expertise, 
which general boards lack (Qiu et al., 2026; Velte, 2017).
In theory and practice, then, sustainability committees serve as more rigorous internal monitors (Velte, 2017).
Stakeholder-agency theory provides another perspective.
An active sustainability committee can help firms achieve substantive ESG-related goals instead of symbolic 
gestures (Ştefănescu & Suciu, 2026).
This reduces the likelihood of brownwashing through consistent communication with stakeholders and setting 
concrete ESG targets.
Based on this we propose the third hypothesis: Hypothesis 3 (H3).
A board-level sustainability committee correlates negatively with corporate brownwashing.
Second, the board-level sustainability committee's role in ESG reporting extends beyond direct oversight.
The committee can act as a catalyst for more formalized ESG reporting.
Resource dependence theory suggests that the presence of a board-level sustainability committee predicts a 
firm's willingness to adopt GRI reporting guidelines.
Covering as many indicator categories as GRI does requires specialized expertise to collect and disclose 
multi-dimensional data.
A sustainability committee helps meet that requirement by bringing directors with relevant experience onto the 
board (Qiu et al., 2026), and by keeping non-financial accountability on the agenda, it pushes the board toward 
formal, indicator-based reporting.
We therefore expect listed firms with such a committee to be more likely to adopt GRI standards:
Hypothesis 4 (H4).
The presence of a board-level sustainability committee correlates positively with the likelihood of GRI adoption.

We obtain the 2014-2024 ASEAN-5 sample from three databases: we derive ESG data from LSEG (London 
Stock Exchange Group) Workspace, obtain governance data from BoardEx and official corporate disclosures, 
and extract financial data from Compustat Global.
The estimation sample consists of 40,799 firm-years observed over 3,709 firms.
We exclude observations with missing values, financial firms, and firms not reporting CO2 emissions, which

Main results focus on two way decoupling of ESG reporting practices in ASEAN 5 markets during 2014 2024 
by jointly considering the effects of external reporting guidance and internal board monitoring on corporate 
brownwashing.
Our panel fixed effects findings indicate a consistent pattern.
The adoption of GRI sustainability reporting standards is negatively and significantly associated with 
brownwashing but in a non homogeneous manner.
GRI standards restrict both the extensive margin of brownwashing decisions i e whether a firm initiates 
brownwashing and the intensive margin i e the severity of ESG score underreporting among brownwashers with 
coefficients similar in magnitude but the extensive margin effect estimated with greater statistical precision.
This pattern is congruent with the information discipline hypothesis H1.
Uniform comparable indicators are likely to increase the reputational risks associated with brownwashing Garcia 
Sanchez et al 2022 Kim Lyon 2015 and act as a deterrent against the initiation of brownwashing practices and with 
less precision against its continuation once underway though this effect is more muted for voluntary standards 
practiced in the developing ASEAN markets Boiral 2013 Roszkowska Menkes et al 2024.
Second neither FemD nor SustCom correlates significantly with brownwashing H2 H3 which runs against 
stakeholder agency and gender socialization theories but fits a tokenism explanation instead Kanter 1977 Manita 
et al 2018 women still hold only 18 97 of ASEAN boardroom seats on average short of the critical mass needed 
to affect disclosure decisions Moreno Ureba 2026 and sustainability committees may function as a legitimacy 
seeking symbol rather than a substantive monitor Qiu et al 2026.
Third the antecedent model results indicate that sustainability committee presence is not a significant predictor 
of the adoption of GRI guidelines.
