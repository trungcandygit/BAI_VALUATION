## Comment 1: First recorded score and publicly observable coverage

*"My main concern is whether the first year in which an LSEG ESG score appears in the database represents a meaningful treatment event. *This distinction is particularly important because the study seeks to estimate the valuation consequences of entering ESG coverage. A score recorded retrospectively in a database is not necessarily information that was available to investors at that time. If the actual publication date differs across firms, the estimated treatment effects may combine pre-publication and post-publication observations.*

*The authors attempt to address this issue by shifting the treatment year forward by one or two years. While these exercises are useful, they do not establish when investors could first observe the scores.*

*I encourage the authors to investigate whether historical coverage information or archived score publication dates can be obtained, at least for a subset of firms. We agree, and we have revised the manuscript and the claims throughout. We could not date publication with the data we hold, so we now distinguish the first recorded score from publicly observable coverage in every section and draw conclusions only where they hold across datings.

We found no public archive of the provider's coverage lists for the five markets. ## Comment 2: Conditional parallel trends

*"The validity of the difference-in-differences estimates depends heavily on the conditional parallel trends assumption. However, the empirical evidence raises concerns about whether this assumption is reasonable in the present setting.*

*Section 5.3 reports that firms entering ESG coverage experienced approximately 10% greater growth in their market-to-book ratios before the recorded treatment year. Although the joint pre-trend test for the primary outcome does not reject at the 5% level (p = 0.074), this result should not be interpreted as evidence supporting parallel trends. The significant pre-treatment differences at individual event years and the results for market capitalization warrant particular attention.*

*The placebo exercise in Table 3 (R5) also produces a significant positive estimate for market-to-book ratios. I recognize that the authors correctly describe this result as another manifestation of the pre-coverage growth pattern rather than independent evidence. Nevertheless, it reinforces the difficulty of constructing a credible counterfactual for the treated firms.*

*The sensitivity analyses in Section 5.4 are helpful, but they also show that relatively modest departures from parallel trends can materially change the interpretation of the results. I would like the authors to explain more clearly why conditional parallel trends might hold after the base year despite the observed differences in earlier growth trajectories.*

*The paper would also benefit from a clearer distinction between the observed evolution of valuation differences and the causal effect attributed to ESG coverage. If the identification assumptions cannot be supported convincingly, the conclusions should place greater emphasis on the descriptive evidence concerning selection into coverage."*

We agree with each point and have revised the text accordingly. The pooled sample fails the pre-trend diagnostic, the post-coverage estimates are now presented as conditional on the parallel trends assumption, and the conclusions rest mainly on the descriptive evidence of selection.

On the pre-trend test, Section 4.1 now states: "The MTB non-rejection is not evidence of parallel trends: the estimates for event years −5 and −4 differ from zero, and pre-trend tests have low power (Roth, 2022)." On the placebo, Section 4.3 now states that its failure restates the pre-coverage growth in Figure 1. On the sensitivity analyses, Section 4.3 now concludes that "departures from parallel trends smaller than those observed before coverage are therefore enough to change the reading of either dating."

On why conditional parallel trends might hold after the base year, a new paragraph in Section 3.3 gives two arguments for and one against. In the Malaysian wave, which carries {{s_w_wave_MY_2021_22}}% of the weight in the pooled estimate, the pre-coverage estimates show no detectable run-up (joint pre-trend p {{mtb_r4wMY_pre_p_txt}}), so for these firms the assumption does not require a trend to stop. ## Comment 7: Uneven distribution across markets

*"Although the study covers five Southeast Asian markets, the distribution of treated firms is highly uneven. Malaysia and Thailand account for the overwhelming majority of firms entering ESG coverage, while the samples for Indonesia and the Philippines are particularly small.*

*The supplementary analyses are useful in showing how the estimates vary across countries. However, the limited number of treated firms in some markets makes it difficult to draw meaningful country-specific conclusions.*

*The Malaysian coverage wave also differs considerably from the other cohorts in terms of pre-treatment valuation dynamics. This raises the question of whether the pooled estimate represents a reasonably coherent treatment effect or averages across substantially different coverage processes.*

*I suggest that the authors discuss more explicitly what the pooled estimates represent, particularly given the concentration of treatment observations in a few markets and years. The interpretation should acknowledge that the results primarily reflect the markets and coverage waves with the greatest representation in the sample."*

We agree. A new part of Section 4.4 on what the pooled estimate represents and a new Table S10 report the weight of each market, wave, and year. Malaysian firms carry {{s_w_market_MY}}% of the weight in the headline MTB estimate and Thai firms {{s_w_market_TH}}%; the Malaysian wave alone carries {{s_w_wave_MY_2021_22}}%, and firm-years from 2021 to 2024 carry {{s_w_years_2021_2024}}%. The section states that "the pooled estimate therefore describes mainly the Malaysian expansion and the Thai cohorts after the COVID-19 pandemic, not an average effect for the five markets," and that the country estimates for Indonesia, the Philippines, and Singapore cannot support conclusions for those markets.

On whether the pooled estimate averages different coverage processes, the revision treats the waves as distinct throughout. Section 4.1 reports the paths by wave, and Section 4.2 and Table S6 show that the waves differ under the later dating as well: the Malaysian wave, in which no run-up was detected, shows no detectable change under either dating, while the waves with a run-up show the decline. ## Comment 8: Framing of the contribution

*"The most convincing contribution of this paper, in my view, is the evidence that entry into ESG rating coverage is selective and may follow substantial changes in firm characteristics and valuation. This is an important issue for empirical studies that compare ESG-rated and unrated firms.*

*The evidence regarding the valuation effect of coverage itself is less conclusive. The baseline estimates are close to zero, but the alternative timing specifications, pre-treatment dynamics, and sensitivity analyses leave room for different interpretations.*

*I therefore encourage the authors to reconsider how the paper's contribution is framed. Rather than presenting the absence of a detectable valuation gain as the central finding, the study could place greater emphasis on the empirical challenges created by selective and potentially retrospective ESG coverage.*

*The implications for investors, rating providers, and regulators should also be reconsidered in this light. In particular, the results do not establish that expanding ESG coverage has no valuation benefits. They indicate that such benefits cannot be reliably identified from the present data and research design without additional assumptions."*

We agree and have reframed the paper. Selection into coverage is now the central finding, and the valuation effect is presented as something the data cannot identify without further assumptions.

The abstract now leads with selection and states that "whether coverage itself changed valuations depends on when investors first saw the score and on untestable trend assumptions." The introduction presents the evidence in this order: entry is selective; after entry the gap stops widening but the data do not pin down a causal effect; and a static regression attributes selection to the score. The contributions are restated as evidence of selection, a demonstration of why the valuation effect is hard to identify, and a warning for empirical work. Section 5 now makes the reviewer's point: "With annual data, a provider that chooses whom to score, and recorded scores that may precede publication, a valuation effect of coverage cannot be reliably identified without additional assumptions." Appendix A.6 of the supplementary material reports that one of many exploratory subgroups, the {{mtb_r4c19_ntr}} firms first scored by 2019, shows a positive estimate that we do not read as evidence of a gain. The title already names coverage selection, so we have kept it.

We have rewritten the implications in Section 5. For researchers, studies of ESG ratings in the region should model when firms enter coverage, report pre-entry dynamics, and record the download date of their rating data. For rating providers and data users, publishing the date on which each firm's coverage began, and keeping the vintages of revised scores, would separate what was known from what was filled in later. For exchanges, regulators, and listed firms, the section now states that "arguments for expanding coverage should therefore rest on grounds other than an expected rise in issuer valuations." The conclusion has been rewritten in the same terms and ends with the data that would move the question forward: point-in-time records of when each firm's score was first published, and ownership and trading data.
