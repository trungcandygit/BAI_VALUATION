Who Gets an ESG Score? Coverage Selection and Firm Value in Five Southeast Asian Markets
Abstract
Most listed firms in Southeast Asia have no environmental, social, and governance (ESG) score, so investors who screen on scores cannot hold them. We ask which firms enter the London Stock Exchange Group (LSEG) ESG database and what happens to their valuation around entry. We compare 675 non-financial firms in Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore, and Thailand that received a first recorded score with 2,613 never-scored firms. Entry is selective. Scored firms are larger, and on average their market-to-book ratio had grown 10.0% more than that of similar firms in the years before their first recorded score, a run-up not detected in a broad Malaysian wave. After entry, the valuation gap stops widening, but whether coverage itself changed valuations depends on when investors first saw the score and on untestable trend assumptions. Research and investment screens that compare rated with unrated firms should model who gets rated and when.
Keywords: ESG ratings, firm valuation, rating coverage, selection, Southeast Asia, sustainable finance
JEL Classification: G14, G15, G32, M14, Q56

1. Introduction
Environmental, social, and governance (ESG) scores work as an entry ticket: a fund, index, or mandate that may hold only scored stocks cannot buy a firm that no provider covers. In five Southeast Asian equity markets, most listed firms had no score from the London Stock Exchange Group (LSEG) at any point between 2014 and 2024, and much of the academic evidence on ESG ratings uses LSEG data (Berg, Kölbel, et al., 2022; Dobrick et al., 2023). This paper asks which firms enter the scored universe and what happens to their market valuation around entry.
If a score makes a firm visible to more investors, its price should rise, because a wider investor base shares its risk (Merton, 1987); if the score also tells investors whether the firm is green or brown, prices should move in opposite directions for the two groups (Pástor et al., 2021; Pedersen et al., 2021). A provider, however, is likely to add a firm once it is large, liquid, and discloses enough to be scored, and its scores rise with firm size (Dobrick et al., 2023; Drempetic et al., 2020). If firms enter after a period of rising value, a comparison of scored and unscored firms credits the score with growth that came before it. Studies, mostly of U.S. firms, link coverage by more raters to fewer ESG violations (Tsang et al., 2024), lower emissions and more ownership by high-ESG institutions (Bikmetova & Pirinsky, 2026), and dividend increases (Tsang et al., 2025). None examines valuation in emerging markets, where most firms are unscored and coverage has expanded in waves.
We study Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore, and Thailand. Of the 675 non-financial firms whose first recorded LSEG score falls between 2015 and 2024, 577 had it in 2020 to 2023, and 289 were Malaysian firms first scored in 2021 or 2022. We compare them with 2,613 never-scored firms before and after each firm's first recorded score, the first fiscal year for which the database holds a score, using the estimator of Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021), which avoids the biases of two-way fixed effects regressions under staggered timing (Baker et al., 2022; Goodman-Bacon, 2021). The first recorded score is not necessarily the date on which investors could first see the score. A pre-analysis plan, committed before the estimation code, records the hypotheses, sample, estimator, and robustness checks; every later analysis is labeled exploratory.
The evidence has three parts. First, entry is selective. Scored firms are larger, and from five years before the first recorded score to the base year (the year before it) their market capitalization grew 14.1% more than that of never-scored firms of similar size, country, and industry (95% interval 3.1% to 25.3%) and their market-to-book (MTB) ratio 10.0% more (1.9% to 18.2%). The run-up is large in the 108 Thai and Singaporean firms first scored in 2019 and 2020 and not detected in the 289 Malaysian firms first scored in 2021 and 2022, the largest wave in the data (95% interval −7.4% to 15.2%). Second, after entry the MTB gap stops widening, but the data do not pin down a causal effect of coverage. Under parallel trends from the base year, the average change in log MTB over the first four years is 0.002 (95% interval −0.046 to 0.048); dated one year later, when a score could first have been public, the change is −6.6%. Most of this difference arises because the later dating measures valuations from the first recorded year, after the cohorts' own rise in that year, and the decline appears only in the waves that had a run-up. Third, a static two-way fixed effects regression on the same data attributes a 6.6% MTB increase to coverage, which disappears once each cohort is compared with its own base year and base-year size is held fixed.
The paper makes three contributions. It documents selection into ESG rating coverage in Southeast Asian markets and adds valuation to the rating-coverage literature (Bikmetova & Pirinsky, 2026; Tsang et al., 2024, 2025). It shows why the valuation effect of coverage is hard to identify: entry follows growth and the recorded first score may precede publication. It also warns that comparisons of scored and unscored firms, and regressions that pool all years around entry, can attribute selection to the score.
2. Related Literature and Hypotheses
2.1 ESG Ratings as Information and Certification
ESG ratings can move prices when they change what investors know or which investors may hold a stock. MSCI downgrades are followed by lower ownership by U.S. funds with ESG mandates and a one-year abnormal return of approximately −2.37%, with weaker effects for upgrades (Berg, Heeb, et al., 2022), and calendar-time portfolios show a similar asymmetry (Shanaev & Ghimire, 2022). Publishing Morningstar sustainability ratings for mutual funds redirected fund flows (Hartzmark & Sussman, 2019).
Certification by inclusion in a sustainability index is the closest analog to a first score. Additions to the Dow Jones Sustainability Index have limited price effects relative to similar firms outside the index (Hawn et al., 2018), and a replication finds no price effect but more analyst following after addition (Durand et al., 2019). In sell-side research, losing analyst coverage lowers prices (Kelly & Ljungqvist, 2012), and the first report on a neglected stock raises its price when the report is favorable (Demiroglu & Ryngaert, 2010).
2.2 ESG Demand, Recognition, and Price Pressure
Two channels predict a valuation response to a first score. Under investor recognition, a stock known to few investors trades at a discount because a small group bears its idiosyncratic risk (Merton, 1987), so recognition predicts a gain for every newly scored firm; S&P 500 additions raise prices permanently, whereas deletions do not lower them permanently (Chen et al., 2004). Under ESG preferences, investors accept lower expected returns on assets they classify as sustainable (Pástor et al., 2021; Pedersen et al., 2021), so a first score predicts gains for firms revealed as green and losses for those revealed as brown, and a pooled average can be close to zero. Both channels work through who trades the stock, and on the Stock Exchange of Thailand local institutional investors adjust their trading to brokerage fees over the long run, whereas foreign and retail investors adjust in the short run (Sudsawasd & Sakdapat, 2026).
Demand shocks also shape realized prices before any rating event. The high realized returns of green assets reflect unexpected increases in ESG concerns rather than high expected returns (Pástor et al., 2022), S&P 500 inclusion raises prices (Harris & Gurel, 1986; Shleifer, 1986), and part of the increase reverses within weeks (Harris & Gurel, 1986). A run-up in value before coverage, followed by a flat path, is consistent with a demand shift that ended, with selection on growth, or with both.
2.3 Which Firms Providers Score
We expect providers to cover firms in the indices their clients track and firms that disclose enough to be scored, although our data omit the provider's coverage rules. Mandatory sustainability reporting increases the information available on listed firms (Krueger et al., 2024), and ownership shapes corporate social responsibility performance: among Vietnamese listed consumer-goods firms, outside our sample, foreign ownership is associated with higher corporate social responsibility scores, and state and family ownership with lower ones (Nguyen, 2025). The provider we study gives higher scores to larger firms (Dobrick et al., 2023; Drempetic et al., 2020) and has rewritten historical scores on a large scale (Berg et al., 2020). Providers disagree about the same firm (Berg, Kölbel, et al., 2022; Christensen et al., 2022), and the disagreement is priced (Avramov et al., 2022; Gibson Brandon et al., 2021). If providers select firms that have grown, a scored-unscored comparison mixes the effect of coverage with the growth that led to it.
2.4 Hypotheses
The pre-analysis plan states three hypotheses, all tested two-sided.
H1 (primary): The initiation of LSEG ESG coverage changes the log MTB ratio of scored firms relative to comparable never-scored firms. Recognition predicts an increase; preferences predict an increase for high-score firms and a decrease for low-score firms; selection predicts relative growth before coverage and, after it, no effect if the growth was permanent or a decline if it was transient.
H2 (secondary): Coverage changes log market capitalization, book leverage, and log total assets.
H3 (heterogeneity): The effect on log MTB differs between firms with initial scores above and below their cohort median. Only the preference channel depends on what the score says, so a difference would point toward it; the absence of a difference cannot rule it out.
Without ownership, fund holding, or trading data, we cannot observe the channels themselves; the data show whether entry follows relative growth, how the valuation gap evolves around entry, and whether that path differs with the initial score.
3. Data and Methods
3.1 Institutional Setting and Sample
The five markets introduced sustainability reporting rules at different times: Bursa Malaysia for financial years ending on or after December 31, 2016, phased in from the largest companies (Bursa Malaysia, 2015); Singapore, on a comply-or-explain basis, from financial years ending on or after December 31, 2017 (Singapore Exchange, 2016); the Philippines from the 2019 annual reports (Securities and Exchange Commission Philippines, 2019); Indonesia, under the original schedule of a 2017 regulation, from 2020 for listed companies other than small- and medium-asset issuers (Otoritas Jasa Keuangan, 2017); and Thailand in the 56-1 One Report from the fiscal year ending in 2021 (Tilleke & Gibbins, 2020). In Malaysia, FTSE Russell, another part of LSEG, scored only the constituents of the FTSE Bursa Malaysia EMAS index, about 30% of listed companies, until Bursa Malaysia and LSEG agreed in November 2022 to extend these scores to all Main and ACE Market companies (Bernama, 2022). The LSEG ESG score we study is a separate product, so our event is the initiation of LSEG ESG coverage, not a firm's first ESG rating of any kind. Coverage arrived in waves that follow no single reporting rule: 108 first scores in Thailand and Singapore in 2019 and 2020, and 289 in Malaysia in 2021 and 2022 (Table S1 and Figure S1 of the supplementary material).
The data combine the annual LSEG ESG score (LSEG, 2025), not the controversy-adjusted combined score, with accounting and market data from Compustat Global (S&P Global Market Intelligence, 2025) for 3,709 listed firms, each with a row for every year from 2014 to 2024; rows before a firm lists carry no data. Every firm in the extract has data in 2024, so the sample conditions on survival to 2024, and without delisting data we cannot sign the resulting bias. Dropping financial firms leaves 3,423 firms. A firm's first recorded score year, g, is its first fiscal year with an LSEG ESG score, and its base year is g − 1. Only 17 of the 675 scored firms have a later year without a score, so we treat coverage as permanent. Excluding the 135 firms scored in 2014, which have no pre-coverage year, leaves 3,288 firms with 32,521 listed firm-years (3,647 rows carry no data, almost all before listing): 675 firms first scored between 2015 and 2024 (59.9% of them Malaysian) and 2,613 never-scored firms, which form the comparison group; Tables S2 and S3 of the supplementary material give the firm-years and firms behind each estimate. The source data contain no MTB ratio at or below zero, so firm-years with negative book equity appear as missing; log MTB ratios are missing for 18.5% of never-scored and 9.0% of scored listed firm-years.
3.2 Event Dating, Outcomes, and Recording Errors
The first recorded score may precede the year in which investors first see a score. The score for fiscal year g draws on reports published after that year ends, so it reaches investors in year g + 1 or later; the provider has rewritten historical scores on a large scale (Berg et al., 2020); scores for the five most recent fiscal years can change after publication (Sahin et al., 2023); and backfilling at entry would widen the gap. The first recorded score can therefore date entry too early but, unless the provider deleted an earlier score, not too late. Our extract holds each score by fiscal year, with no publication or update date, as it stood at download; that every scored firm still has a score in 2024 fits a download of the provider's current universe. LSEG distributes a point-in-time version of its ESG data that keeps values as they were published (LSEG, 2023), but our extract does not contain it. We therefore keep two concepts apart. The first recorded score is what we observe; publicly observable coverage begins at an unknown date from g + 1 onward. We report the valuation path with coverage dated at g, at g + 1, and at g + 2, and draw conclusions only where they hold across these datings.
The primary outcome is the natural logarithm of the MTB ratio. The secondary outcomes are log market capitalization, log total assets, and book leverage, total debt divided by total assets; without stock returns or shares outstanding, we treat market capitalization and total assets as measures of scale. Amounts are in U.S. dollars, winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles of the pooled sample. A few market capitalization records are implausibly large; one Indonesian firm is recorded at 1,138.8 billion U.S. dollars. Because market capitalization divided by the MTB ratio gives the implied book equity, which cannot exceed total assets, we flag firm-years in which the implied book equity exceeds ten times total assets: 209 of 27,127 firm-years, 73 of them for scored firms. A lower threshold, a ratio above two, flags 749 firm-years.

3.4 Inference, Sensitivity, and Robustness Checks
We bootstrap by resampling firms with their full time series; all specifications share the same 999 draws and seed, so differences between specifications have a valid joint distribution. We report the bootstrap standard error (SE), the percentile 95% confidence interval, a normal-approximation p-value, and the minimum detectable effect at 80% power, MDE ≈ 2.8 × SE. Secondary outcomes carry Holm-adjusted p-values. 4. Results
4.1 Who Gets Scored and the Valuation Path Around Entry
Table 1 compares scored firms in the base year with never-scored firm-years. Scored firms are larger: their median market capitalization is 203.2 million U.S. dollars against 42.2 million, and their median total assets are 4.3 times as large. Their MTB ratio is also higher, while leverage differs little. Because scored firms are concentrated in the base years 2019 to 2022, these level differences mix calendar effects with selection. In Malaysia, where the expansion was broadest, the median total assets of scored firms, each firm measured by its median over all sample years, were 3.3 times those of the 565 never-scored firms.

Before the first recorded score, the estimates for market capitalization and MTB are negative and rise toward zero; in event years −5 and −4, the MTB estimates are −0.095 and −0.082 (p = 0.015 and p = 0.015). From event year −5 to the base year, scored firms' market capitalization grew 14.1% more than that of never-scored firms, and their MTB ratio 10.0% more; we call this relative growth the run-up. The joint pre-trend test rejects for market capitalization (p = 0.022) but not at the 5% level for MTB (p = 0.074). The MTB non-rejection is not evidence of parallel trends: the estimates for event years −5 and −4 differ from zero, and pre-trend tests have low power (Roth, 2022). After the first recorded score, the MTB estimates for event years 0 to 3 lie between −0.044 and 0.017, and none differs from zero at the 5% level; event year 4 rests on only 141 scored firms and is excluded from the headline estimate. The MTB gap thus widens before coverage and stops widening afterward. Only reading the post-coverage part as an effect of coverage depends on the parallel trends assumption.

4.2 Average Change After Entry and the Two Datings
Table 2 reports the headline estimates under parallel trends. The change in log MTB is 0.002, with a 95% confidence interval of −0.046 to 0.048, so the data are compatible with changes in the MTB ratio from −4.5% to 4.9%; the MDE is about 7.0%. With the cluster bootstrap, which allows for market-wide shocks such as the political uncertainty that moves Thai stock prices (Prukumpai et al., 2022), the interval is −0.050 to 0.053. After Holm adjustment, no secondary outcome changes significantly at the 5% level: log market capitalization changes by −0.055 (p = 0.134), leverage by 0.8 percentage points (p = 0.134), and log total assets by 0.044 (p = 0.053). The MTB estimate shows no sign of the gain that the recognition channel predicts (Merton, 1987).

The reference year thus drives the difference. Moving it from g − 1 to g subtracts from each later estimate the change in log MTB of the same scored firms, relative to the same never-scored firms, between g − 1 and g. The row 4 step, −0.044, is therefore minus each cohort's own change between g − 1 and g (0.044 on average) weighted as in event years 1 to 4, which fall most on the early cohorts observed longest after entry; it is an accounting identity, not a separate estimate, and it is larger than the pooled event-year-0 estimate in Figure 1. Part of the decline under the later dating is thus the rise these cohorts had in the first recorded year itself. The cohorts observed through event year 4 (rows 7 to 10) show the same pattern more strongly.
The timing difference is also concentrated in the waves that had a run-up. Under the one-year-later dating, the MTB change is −0.136 for the Thai and Singaporean wave and −0.111 for the remaining cohorts, but 0.012 for the Malaysian wave (Table S6 of the supplementary material). Tested with joint firm-bootstrap draws, the Thai and Singaporean wave differs from the Malaysian wave both in the run-up (difference in the event-year −5 estimate −0.258, p = 0.010) and in the one-year-later change (−0.148, p = 0.006). The remaining cohorts differ from the Malaysian wave in the one-year-later change (−0.123, p = 0.010) but not detectably in the run-up (−0.075, 95% interval −0.219 to 0.075). Where no run-up was detected, neither dating detects an MTB change (95% intervals −0.053 to 0.091 and −0.055 to 0.076).
Two readings fit this pattern, and the data cannot separate them. If firms were selected at an unusually high valuation that later partly reversed, the one-year-later estimate measures that reversal and could hide a gain from coverage; if valuations would otherwise have stayed at their peak, coverage lowered them. Dating coverage two years later gives −0.048 (95% interval −0.099 to 0.000), and Figure S2 plots the one-year-later path.
4.3 Sensitivity and Robustness
Keeping only the 7 cohorts observed through event year 3 gives −0.008 (p = 0.806), so changing cohort composition does not explain the headline estimate. Removing a linear pre-trend gives −0.052, and adding pre-coverage growth as a covariate gives 0.028; both intervals include zero. Under the relative-magnitude bounds (Table S7), with M̄ = 0.25 the interval for the MTB effect is −0.071 to 0.073, and any departure with M̄ above 0.01 admits a 5% increase. Under the one-year-later dating, the decline loses significance with M̄ = 0.25, when its interval runs from −0.138 to 0.002. Departures from parallel trends smaller than those observed before coverage are therefore enough to change the reading of either dating.

4.4 Initial Scores and What the Pooled Estimate Represents
The last three rows of Table 3 divide scored firms at the cohort median of their first recorded ESG score. The difference between high- and low-score entrants is −0.041 (standard error 0.040, p = 0.307). Because the score rises with firm size (correlation 0.35 with base-year log total assets), Table S4 repeats the split after purging the score of size, country, and cohort; the difference is then −0.027 (p = 0.491). Neither split shows the sign pattern that the preference channel predicts, but with minimum detectable differences of 0.113 and 0.111, about 12% in the MTB ratio, neither can detect a moderate difference, and the residual split removes only part of this link, so the result does not show that ESG preferences are unimportant.
The pooled estimate weights cohorts and event years by the number of scored firms in each cell. Malaysian firms carry 55.2% of the weight in the headline MTB estimate and Thai firms 26.7%, together 81.9%; the Malaysian wave of 2021 and 2022 alone carries 44.1%, and firm-years from 2021 to 2024 carry 87.2% (Table S10). The pooled estimate therefore describes mainly the Malaysian expansion and the Thai cohorts after the COVID-19 pandemic, not an average effect for the five markets; the estimates for Indonesia, the Philippines, and Singapore in Table S11 rest on too few scored firms to support conclusions. Under the parallel trends assumption, the largest increase compatible with the headline interval is of the same order as the announcement return of 4.86% that Demiroglu and Ryngaert (2010) report for the first analyst coverage of neglected stocks, and because annual ratios miss short-lived price pressure, such as the price rise at S&P 500 inclusion (Shleifer, 1986), part of which reverses within weeks (Harris & Gurel, 1986), our estimates concern lasting revaluation (Chen et al., 2004). A first score also differs from the loss of analyst coverage, which lowers prices (Kelly & Ljungqvist, 2012): we find no comparable valuation response to gaining coverage.
5. Discussion and Conclusion
The clearest result is an ordering of events: firms entered the scored universe after their MTB ratio and market capitalization had grown faster than those of never-scored firms, and their MTB ratio stopped gaining afterward. The provider may have added firms once they were large and visible enough to matter to its clients, consistent with its higher scores for larger firms (Dobrick et al., 2023; Drempetic et al., 2020), or firms may have become scoreable by reporting, which mandatory disclosure rules make more likely (Krueger et al., 2024), by joining an index, or by issuing equity; the fall in leverage toward the base year fits equity issuance. This ordering does not rest on parallel trends or on the dating of publication, although its size depends on conditioning on base-year size: without covariates, the MTB run-up is 6.6% (pre-trend p = 0.028). Whether coverage then changed valuations depends on the counterfactual trend (Rambachan & Roth, 2023; Roth, 2022): with parallel paths, the data show no detected effect (95% interval −0.046 to 0.048 in log MTB); with a continued run-up, coverage lowered valuations; with a partly transitory run-up, a positive effect could have offset a reversal. With annual data, a provider that chooses whom to score, and recorded scores that may precede publication, a valuation effect of coverage cannot be reliably identified without additional assumptions.
The absence of a detected gain fits the evidence on certification by sustainability indices (Durand et al., 2019; Hawn et al., 2018) and contrasts with the price effects of MSCI rating changes (Berg, Heeb, et al., 2022; Shanaev & Ghimire, 2022). U.S. ESG fund holdings respond to MSCI ratings, not to those of other providers (Berg, Heeb, et al., 2022); ratings that investors see directly, such as Morningstar's fund sustainability ratings, redirect flows (Hartzmark & Sussman, 2019); and because providers disagree about the same firm and the disagreement is priced (Avramov et al., 2022; Berg, Kölbel, et al., 2022; Christensen et al., 2022; Gibson Brandon et al., 2021), entry into one provider's coverage need not change how investors who rely on other ratings value a firm. Scored firms were also large and visible before coverage, which leaves less room for recognition (Merton, 1987). Unlike U.S. evidence that coverage changes firm conduct and payout (Bikmetova & Pirinsky, 2026; Tsang et al., 2024, 2025), we find no detected change in leverage (0.8 percentage points, Holm p = 0.134) and a total-assets increase that loses significance after Holm adjustment. The run-up fits evidence that realized green returns reflect unexpected shifts in ESG demand (Pástor et al., 2022), but without ownership data we cannot tell whether ESG investors bought these firms before or after coverage; since ownership shapes corporate social responsibility performance in the region (Nguyen, 2025) and Thai institutional, foreign, and retail investors trade on different horizons (Sudsawasd & Sakdapat, 2026), ownership and trading data by investor group are the natural next step.
For researchers, comparisons of scored and unscored firms, static TWFE estimates, and backtests built on the first recorded score can attribute selection to the score (Berg et al., 2020; Sahin et al., 2023), so studies of ESG ratings in Southeast Asia should model when firms enter coverage, report pre-entry dynamics, and record the download date of their rating data. For providers, publishing the date on which each firm's coverage began, with the vintages of revised scores, would separate what was known from what was filled in later. For investors, a portfolio screened on ESG scores in these markets holds firms that, on average, entered coverage after outgrowing their peers. For exchanges, regulators, and listed firms, broad expansions such as the Malaysian wave appear to select less on prior growth, and we detect no MTB gain within them, although the interval (−0.053 to 0.091) admits gains larger than the pooled upper bound; arguments for expanding coverage should therefore rest on grounds other than an expected rise in issuer valuations.
The study has limitations. The post-coverage estimates rest on conditional parallel trends that the pre-coverage estimates question, and our bound is a simplified version of Rambachan and Roth (2023). We observe neither publication dates, returns, ownership, nor index membership, and only LSEG coverage, and flagging implausible market capitalization removes order-of-magnitude recording errors but not smaller ones. MTB ratios are missing for 18.5% of never-scored and 9.0% of scored listed firm-years, Malaysian and Thai firms make up 84.3% of scored firms, the sample includes only firms that survived to 2024, and effects smaller than the MDE of 0.067 on log MTB would be detected with less than 80% probability.
In conclusion, entry into LSEG ESG coverage in five Southeast Asian markets was selective. Scored firms were larger than never-scored firms, and on average their market-to-book ratio had grown 10.0% more than that of similar firms from five years to one year before entry, most in the Thai and Singaporean wave of 2019 and 2020 and not detectably in the broad Malaysian wave of 2021 and 2022 (95% interval −7.4% to 15.2%). After entry, the valuation gap stopped widening, but whether coverage itself changed valuations cannot be settled: the answer moves from no detected change to a decline of 6.6% depending on when investors are assumed to have seen the score. Research and investment screens that start from a provider's universe inherit the provider's selection, and point-in-time records of first publication, with ownership and trading data, would allow future work to test whether ESG coverage changes who holds a firm and at what price.
