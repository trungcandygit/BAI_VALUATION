Southeast Asian Journal of Economics

Thank you for the opportunity to revise our manuscript, and thank you and Reviewer A for comments that were specific enough to act on. We have revised the paper in response to every comment. First, we now distinguish throughout between the first recorded score, which the database shows, and publicly observable coverage, which we cannot date, and a new Section 3.3 explains what the recorded year can and cannot tell us. Response to the Editor
Comment E1
“Emphasis on originality and clearer contribution: Please revise the paper in a way that makes its contribution clearer for the reader. You need to motivate your paper in relation to the literature and rewrite wherever possible using simple economic intuitions, including the introduction, to clearly state the purpose of the paper and additional contributions. Please also have a look at past issues of the Southeast Asian Journal of Economics and cite papers that are relevant to your topic.”
We agree and have rewritten the introduction around one question and its economic intuition. The revised manuscript changes Section 1 throughout and adds citations to three papers published in the journal (Sections 2.2, 2.3, and 4.3).
The first paragraph now states the purpose in plain terms: an ESG score works as an entry ticket, because funds and indices restricted to scored stocks cannot buy an unscored firm, and the paper asks which firms enter the scored universe and what happens to their valuation around entry. The second paragraph gives the intuition. If a score makes a firm visible to more investors, its price should rise because a wider investor base shares its risk (Merton, 1987); if the score also signals whether the firm is green or brown, prices should move in opposite directions for the two groups. A provider does not score firms at random, however, and if firms enter after a period of rising value, a comparison of scored and unscored firms credits the score with growth that came before it. The paper’s purpose is to separate the effect of being scored from the selection of who is scored. The findings paragraphs now follow this logic, and the contribution paragraph states three contributions: evidence of selection into coverage in an emerging-market setting, a demonstration of why the valuation effect of coverage is hard to identify, and a practical warning for empirical work in the region.
We searched past issues of the journal for related work and cite three papers where they bear on our argument. Nguyen (2025) shows that ownership structure shapes corporate social responsibility scores of Vietnamese listed firms; we cite it in Section 2.3 on which firms disclose enough to be scored. Sudsawasd and Sakdapat (2026) show that local institutional, foreign, and retail investors on the Stock Exchange of Thailand respond differently to trading costs; we cite it in Sections 2.2 and 6.3, where investor participation is the channel through which a score would act and which our data cannot observe. Prukumpai et al. (2022) show that political uncertainty moves Thai stock prices; we cite it in Section 4.3 to motivate the market-level shocks that our country indicators and cluster bootstrap address. We did not find a paper in the journal on ESG rating coverage itself, and we did not add citations that are not related to the argument.
Comment E2
“Abstract: Please write the abstract as clearly as possible, along with the key economic arguments for the ideas you are exploring (you should try to keep it at about 150 words or less). Sometimes when the abstract is hard to comprehend or remains technical, an average reader avoids reading the paper. Thus, it would be in your own interest to make the abstract clear, including the last line as a general forward-looking conclusion on the topic.”
We have rewritten the abstract. It now has 144 words, contains no test statistics or confidence intervals, states the economic argument, and ends with a forward-looking conclusion. The revised abstract reads:
Most listed firms in Southeast Asia have no environmental, social, and governance (ESG) score, so investors who screen on scores cannot hold them. We ask which firms enter the London Stock Exchange Group (LSEG) ESG database and what happens to their valuation around entry. We follow 675 non-financial firms in Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore, and Thailand that received a first recorded score and compare them with 2,613 never-scored firms. Entry is selective. Scored firms are larger, and outside a broad Malaysian wave their market-to-book ratio had outgrown that of similar firms before the first recorded score, by 10.0% on average. After entry, the valuation gap stops widening, but whether coverage itself changed valuations depends on when investors first saw the score and on untestable trend assumptions. Research and investment screens that compare rated with unrated firms should model who gets rated and when.

Response to Reviewer A
We thank the reviewer for a careful and constructive report. The reviewer’s main point, that the evidence supports selection into coverage more firmly than a valuation effect of coverage, is correct, and the revision is organized around it. We respond to each comment in turn.
Comment 1: First recorded score and publicly observable coverage
“My main concern is whether the first year in which an LSEG ESG score appears in the database represents a meaningful treatment event. Section 3.2 acknowledges that the recorded fiscal year may precede the actual publication of the score and that historical scores may have been backfilled or subsequently revised.
This distinction is particularly important because the study seeks to estimate the valuation consequences of entering ESG coverage. A score recorded retrospectively in a database is not necessarily information that was available to investors at that time. If the actual publication date differs across firms, the estimated treatment effects may combine pre-publication and post-publication observations.
The authors attempt to address this issue by shifting the treatment year forward by one or two years. While these exercises are useful, they do not establish when investors could first observe the scores.
I encourage the authors to investigate whether historical coverage information or archived score publication dates can be obtained, at least for a subset of firms. If this is not feasible, the paper should distinguish more carefully between the first recorded ESG score and the actual initiation of publicly observable ESG coverage. The interpretation of the treatment effects and the claims made in the abstract and conclusion should reflect this distinction.”
We agree, and we have revised the manuscript and the claims throughout. We could not obtain archived publication dates, so we now distinguish the first recorded score from publicly observable coverage in every section and draw conclusions only where they hold across datings.
We looked for the dates. Our extract of LSEG Workspace contains each score by fiscal year, with no publication or update date, and the scores it holds are those in the database at download, after any revision. We found no public archive of the provider’s coverage lists for the five markets, and the provider’s methodology documents describe how scores are updated, not when each firm entered coverage. We therefore cannot date publication for any subset of firms, and the revised paper says so.
The revision makes four changes. First, the event is now called the first recorded score throughout, defined as the first fiscal year for which the database holds a score (Sections 1 and 3.2). Second, a new Section 3.3, “What the first recorded score dates,” explains why the recorded year can precede publication and states the one property we can establish: publication occurs in year g + 1 or later and never before, so the recorded year dates entry too early, never too late. It reads in part: “We therefore keep two concepts apart. The first recorded score is what we observe; publicly observable coverage begins at an unknown date from g + 1 onward. We report the valuation path with coverage dated at g, at g + 1, and at g + 2, and we draw conclusions only where they hold across these datings.” Third, Section 6.1 notes that the selection finding does not depend on the dating, because the run-up occurs before the first recorded score and therefore before any possible publication. Fourth, the abstract and the conclusion no longer state a post-coverage estimate as a valuation effect. The abstract now says that “whether coverage itself changed valuations depends on when investors first saw the score and on untestable trend assumptions,” and the conclusion says that the answer “moves from no detected change to a decline of 6.6% depending on when investors are assumed to have seen the score.”
We agree that later dating does not establish when investors saw the scores. Its value in the revision is different: Section 5.4 uses it to show what changes when the event moves later, and Comment 3 below describes the result.
Comment 2: Conditional parallel trends
“The validity of the difference-in-differences estimates depends heavily on the conditional parallel trends assumption. However, the empirical evidence raises concerns about whether this assumption is reasonable in the present setting.
Section 5.3 reports that firms entering ESG coverage experienced approximately 10% greater growth in their market-to-book ratios before the recorded treatment year. Although the joint pre-trend test for the primary outcome does not reject at the 5% level (p = 0.074), this result should not be interpreted as evidence supporting parallel trends. The significant pre-treatment differences at individual event years and the results for market capitalization warrant particular attention.
The placebo exercise in Table 3 (R5) also produces a significant positive estimate for market-to-book ratios. I recognize that the authors correctly describe this result as another manifestation of the pre-coverage growth pattern rather than independent evidence. Nevertheless, it reinforces the difficulty of constructing a credible counterfactual for the treated firms.
The sensitivity analyses in Section 5.4 are helpful, but they also show that relatively modest departures from parallel trends can materially change the interpretation of the results. I would like the authors to explain more clearly why conditional parallel trends might hold after the base year despite the observed differences in earlier growth trajectories.
The paper would also benefit from a clearer distinction between the observed evolution of valuation differences and the causal effect attributed to ESG coverage. If the identification assumptions cannot be supported convincingly, the conclusions should place greater emphasis on the descriptive evidence concerning selection into coverage.”
We agree with each point and have revised the text accordingly. The pooled sample fails the pre-trend diagnostic, the post-coverage estimates are now presented as conditional on assumption (2), and the conclusions rest mainly on the descriptive evidence of selection.
On the pre-trend test, Section 5.2 now states: “The MTB non-rejection is not evidence of parallel trends: the individual estimates for event years −5 and −4 differ from zero, the market capitalization test rejects, and pre-trend tests have low power (Roth, 2022). We therefore read the pooled sample as failing the pre-trend diagnostic.” On the placebo, Section 5.8 now adds that the failed placebo shows “how hard it is to construct a counterfactual path for firms that were growing toward the provider’s universe.” On the sensitivity analyses, Section 5.5 now concludes that “modest departures from parallel trends, smaller than those observed before coverage, are therefore enough to change the reading of either dating.”
On why conditional parallel trends might hold after the base year, a new paragraph in Section 4.1 gives two arguments for and one against. In the Malaysian wave, which carries 44.1% of the weight in the pooled estimate, the pre-coverage path is flat (joint pre-trend p = 0.549), so for these firms the assumption asks only that a flat relative path stays flat. If the provider adds firms once they cross a size or visibility threshold, relative growth stops being a reason for selection once the threshold is crossed, and conditioning on base-year size compares firms on the same side of it. Against these, if part of the run-up was transitory, scored firms would have fallen back without a score, and the estimates would understate any effect. The paragraph ends: “We therefore treat the post-coverage estimates as conditional on assumption (2) and give most weight to evidence that does not depend on it: the ordering of growth and entry, and the contrast between waves with and without a run-up.”
On the distinction between observed evolution and causal effect, the text now separates the two wherever results are reported. Section 5.2, titled “The valuation path before and after the first recorded score,” describes the observed gap and adds: “This description of the observed gap does not depend on assumption (2); reading the post-coverage part as an effect of coverage does.” Section 5.3 is titled “Average change after entry under assumption (2),” and Table 2 is retitled in the same way. The emphasis of the conclusions has moved to selection, as described under Comment 8.
Comment 3: Baseline and one-year-later datings
“The difference between the baseline estimate and the alternative treatment timing specification deserves more attention.
The baseline estimate for log market-to-book is 0.002, whereas shifting the treatment date forward by one year produces an estimate of −0.068 (Table 3, R10). The latter estimate is statistically significant and economically meaningful.
I acknowledge the authors’ discussion of possible mean reversion and the changing composition of cohorts across event years. However, the two specifications appear to support rather different interpretations of what happens around the initiation of ESG coverage.
It would be useful to disentangle the effects of changing the treatment date, changing the reference period, and changing the estimation sample. In particular, comparisons using a common set of firms and cohorts would help establish whether the differences arise primarily from treatment timing or sample composition.
The authors should also explain which treatment definition is most defensible from an economic perspective. Given the uncertainty surrounding actual score publication dates, the baseline specification should not automatically receive greater weight simply because it was pre-specified.”
We agree and have carried out the decomposition the reviewer suggests. The new Section 5.4 and Table 4 move from the first-recorded-year estimate to the one-year-later estimate one step at a time on a common set of firms, and Table S10 repeats both datings by coverage wave.
Each row of Table 4 changes one feature. Restricting to firms observed in both g − 1 and g, so that the same firms enter every later row, changes the MTB estimate by −0.005. Averaging event years 1 to 4 instead of 0 to 3, with the reference year unchanged, changes it by −0.016 (standard error 0.009). Moving the reference year from g − 1 to g, which is what a one-year-later dating does to the comparison, changes it by −0.044 (standard error 0.019), 62% of the total gap of −0.070. Measuring size in g and returning to all firms change little (0.002 and −0.008). Rows 7 to 10 repeat the steps for the cohorts observed through event year 4 and show the same pattern. The difference between the two estimates therefore comes mainly from the reference period, not from sample composition. Its economic meaning is that the later dating compares every later valuation with the first recorded year, the last year of the run-up: in the cohorts that contribute to event years 1 to 4, the MTB ratio of scored firms rose by a further 0.044 relative to never-scored firms between g − 1 and g, and moving the reference year subtracts exactly this rise.
The wave comparison in Table S10 adds the most informative evidence. Under the one-year-later dating the MTB change is −0.136 for the Thai and Singaporean wave and −0.111 for the remaining cohorts, both of which had a run-up, but 0.012 for the Malaysian wave, which had none; its first-recorded-year estimate is 0.021. Where valuations did not run up before entry, both datings show no MTB change.
On which definition is most defensible, we agree that the baseline should not receive more weight because it was pre-specified, and Section 5.3 now says so: “pre-specification guards against choosing a dating after seeing results, but it does not make the recorded year the date on which investors learned of coverage. The later dating is closer to that date on economic grounds, while the first recorded year is the date we observe.” We report both estimates as answers to different questions. Neither isolates an effect of coverage, because the later dating measures valuations from the top of the run-up. Section 5.4 states the two readings, a reversal of the run-up that could hide a gain or a negative effect of coverage, and that the data cannot separate them. What the datings share is that no specification shows a gain and that the decline appears only where a run-up preceded entry.
Comment 4: Common support and size adjustment
“The paper documents substantial differences between firms that receive ESG scores and those that remain unscored. According to Table 1, scored firms are considerably larger, and Section 4.2 reports that 24.6% of treated firms have base-year total assets exceeding the 95th percentile of the never-scored firms.
This raises concerns about common support and the extent to which the control firms provide a credible counterfactual for the treated firms.
The decision to use outcome regression rather than the doubly robust estimator is understandable given the reported overlap difficulties. However, outcome regression does not eliminate the underlying support problem and may rely heavily on functional-form assumptions when treated firms lie outside the observed range of the controls.
The additional analysis restricting treated firms to the control group’s size range is therefore important. I suggest giving this exercise greater prominence in the main text and reporting more information about the characteristics of the firms retained and excluded.
The authors should also clarify how sensitive the results are to the specification of the size adjustment. This is especially relevant because firm size is closely related to both the probability of receiving ESG coverage and the valuation outcomes being examined.”
We agree. The size-range restriction is now in the main text with a new Section 5.6, “Firm size and common support,” and two new tables. Section 4.2 now states that outcome regression “extrapolates the relation estimated among controls to scored firms larger than any control.”
Table 5 varies the size adjustment and the support. Across functional forms (no covariates, linear, quadratic, cubic, quintile indicators, and size measured at g − 3), the MTB estimate stays between −0.010 and 0.025. Keeping scored firms no larger than the 95th percentile of controls in their cell retains 487 scored firms and gives −0.021 (p = 0.426); the 90th and 99th percentile cut-offs give −0.013 and −0.006. Within the restricted support, the doubly robust estimator becomes usable and gives −0.010 (p = 0.715), with 1.1% of scored firm-cells having an estimated propensity score above 0.95 (Crump et al., 2009; Sant’Anna & Zhao, 2020). The MTB result therefore does not depend on extrapolating the size adjustment.
Market capitalization is more sensitive, as the reviewer anticipates: its estimate is 0.016 without covariates and between −0.096 and −0.033 with them. Section 5.6 now says that the negative market capitalization change depends on how size is modeled.
Table 6 describes the firms retained and excluded. The 138 excluded firms are large (median base-year total assets of 2351.7 million U.S. dollars against 170.9 million) and more often Thai or Indonesian, whereas 71.5% of the retained firms are Malaysian. The text states that the restricted estimate describes mid-sized scored firms, largely from the Malaysian expansion, and says little about the largest firms.
Comment 5: Mechanisms
“The discussion draws on investor recognition and ESG preference theories to motivate the expected valuation effects of ESG coverage. These are reasonable theoretical starting points, but the empirical analysis does not directly observe the mechanisms through which coverage might affect firm value.
In particular, the study does not contain information on institutional ownership, ESG-related fund holdings, trading activity, or changes in investor participation. It is therefore difficult to determine whether the absence of a detectable valuation increase reflects limited investor recognition, weak demand for ESG-rated firms, or other factors.
The high-score versus low-score comparison in Section 5.6 also requires a more cautious interpretation. The insignificant difference between the two groups does not necessarily imply that ESG preferences are unimportant. The test has limited power to detect moderate differences, and the initial ESG score may be correlated with firm characteristics such as size.
I suggest that the authors distinguish more explicitly between theoretical mechanisms that motivate the hypotheses and mechanisms that can actually be evaluated with the available data. The empirical evidence appears better suited to examining selection into ESG coverage and subsequent valuation patterns than to distinguishing between investor recognition and ESG preference channels.”
We agree and have revised three places. A new paragraph at the end of Section 2.4 separates the motivating mechanisms from what the data can evaluate: “The two channels motivate these predictions, but our data cannot observe them. They contain no ownership, fund holdings, trading activity, or investor participation, so we cannot tell whether a missing valuation response reflects weak recognition, weak demand for ESG-rated firms, or something else. What the data can evaluate is narrower: whether entry follows relative growth, how the valuation gap evolves around entry, and whether that path differs with the initial score.” The wording of H3, which the original version described as a test that “separates” the preference channel, now says that a difference would point toward that channel and that the absence of one cannot rule it out. A new Section 6.3, “Mechanisms that the data can and cannot evaluate,” applies the same distinction to the discussion.
The interpretation of the high- and low-score comparison (now Section 5.9) is more cautious. It reports the minimum detectable differences of 0.113 and 0.111, about 11.9% in the MTB ratio, notes that the score is correlated with size (0.35) and that the residual split removes only part of this link, and concludes: “The insignificant difference therefore does not show that ESG preferences are unimportant; our sample cannot detect them if they are moderate.”
Comment 6: Missing market-to-book ratios and implausible market capitalization
“Section 3.2 reports substantial differences in the availability of market-to-book ratios between scored and never-scored firms. The supplementary material further indicates that approximately one-quarter of firm-year observations are missing for the primary outcome.
This is potentially important because missing market-to-book observations may be systematically associated with financial distress, negative book equity, firm size, or the likelihood of receiving ESG coverage.
The authors should provide a more detailed assessment of whether the missing observations affect the composition of the treatment and comparison groups. A comparison of firms with and without usable market-to-book data would be informative.
I am also concerned by the acknowledgment in Section 7 that some market capitalization observations are implausibly large. Winsorization may reduce the influence of extreme observations, but it does not necessarily correct underlying data errors. The authors should explain how these observations were identified and whether the main results are robust to excluding potentially erroneous records.”
We agree that missing ratios may be systematic, and our checks confirm it. A new Section 5.7, new Table 7, and new Table S11 address both concerns. In checking this comment we also corrected a statement in the original Section 3.2: the source data contain no MTB ratio at or below zero, so firm-years with negative book equity appear as missing together with firm-years that lack market or accounting data, and we cannot separate the two. The revised Section 3.2 says so.
Table S11 compares firm-years with and without an MTB ratio. Firm-years without one are more often highly levered (leverage above 0.5 in 25.4% of never-scored firm-years without a ratio, against 8.4% with one) and more often Indonesian (38.9% against 24.1%), consistent with financial distress and with gaps in market data. Only 21.5% of never-scored and 24.8% of scored firm-years without a ratio have a market capitalization, so most gaps are missing market data rather than negative book equity alone. The 50 scored firms that do not enter the MTB estimate have median base-year total assets similar to those that do (280.3 against 294.3 million U.S. dollars), so the missing ratios do not remove the largest or smallest scored firms.
Table 7 tests whether the gaps change the estimates. Keeping only firms with a complete MTB series gives 0.008, and keeping only scored firms with complete series gives 0.006, against the baseline 0.002. Estimating market capitalization only on firm-years that have an MTB ratio moves its change from −0.055 to −0.023, so part of the negative market capitalization estimate comes from firm-years that drop out of the MTB sample; Section 5.7 reports this.
On the implausible market capitalization records, Section 3.4 now explains the rule we use to identify them. Market capitalization divided by the MTB ratio gives the implied book equity, which cannot exceed total assets. Because ratios slightly above one can arise from different measurement dates, we flag firm-years in which the implied book equity exceeds ten times total assets, an error of an order of magnitude. The rule flags 209 of 27,127 firm-years (0.77%), 73 of them for scored firms; the largest flagged value is an Indonesian firm recorded at 1138.8 billion U.S. dollars. Removing the flagged firm-years gives −0.007 for MTB and −0.045 for market capitalization. Removing every firm with a flagged year, flagging at twice instead of ten times total assets, or winsorizing within each market and year gives MTB estimates between −0.019 and 0.005. None of these checks produces an MTB gain. Section 7 now describes the remaining limitation.
Comment 7: Uneven distribution across markets
“Although the study covers five Southeast Asian markets, the distribution of treated firms is highly uneven. Malaysia and Thailand account for the overwhelming majority of firms entering ESG coverage, while the samples for Indonesia and the Philippines are particularly small.
The supplementary analyses are useful in showing how the estimates vary across countries. However, the limited number of treated firms in some markets makes it difficult to draw meaningful country-specific conclusions.
The Malaysian coverage wave also differs considerably from the other cohorts in terms of pre-treatment valuation dynamics. This raises the question of whether the pooled estimate represents a reasonably coherent treatment effect or averages across substantially different coverage processes.
I suggest that the authors discuss more explicitly what the pooled estimates represent, particularly given the concentration of treatment observations in a few markets and years. The interpretation should acknowledge that the results primarily reflect the markets and coverage waves with the greatest representation in the sample.”
We agree. A new Section 5.10, “What the pooled estimate represents, and its magnitude,” and a new Table S9 report the weight of each market, wave, and year. Malaysian firms carry 55.2% of the weight in the headline MTB estimate and Thai firms 26.7%; the Malaysian wave alone carries 44.1%, and firm-years from 2021 to 2024 carry 87.2%. The section states that “the pooled estimate therefore describes mainly the Malaysian expansion and the Thai cohorts in the years after the COVID-19 pandemic,” that it is not an average effect for the five markets, and that the country estimates for Indonesia, the Philippines, and Singapore cannot support conclusions for those markets.
On whether the pooled estimate averages different coverage processes, the revision treats the waves as distinct throughout. Section 5.2 reports the paths by wave, and Section 5.4 and Table S10 show that the waves differ under the later dating as well: the Malaysian wave, which had no run-up, shows no change under either dating, while the waves with a run-up show the decline. We now use this contrast as evidence (Sections 4.1 and 5.4) rather than averaging over it.
Comment 8: Framing of the contribution
“The most convincing contribution of this paper, in my view, is the evidence that entry into ESG rating coverage is selective and may follow substantial changes in firm characteristics and valuation. This is an important issue for empirical studies that compare ESG-rated and unrated firms.
The evidence regarding the valuation effect of coverage itself is less conclusive. The baseline estimates are close to zero, but the alternative timing specifications, pre-treatment dynamics, and sensitivity analyses leave room for different interpretations.
I therefore encourage the authors to reconsider how the paper’s contribution is framed. Rather than presenting the absence of a detectable valuation gain as the central finding, the study could place greater emphasis on the empirical challenges created by selective and potentially retrospective ESG coverage.
The implications for investors, rating providers, and regulators should also be reconsidered in this light. In particular, the results do not establish that expanding ESG coverage has no valuation benefits. They indicate that such benefits cannot be reliably identified from the present data and research design without additional assumptions.”
We agree and have reframed the paper. Selection into coverage is now the central finding, and the valuation effect is presented as something the data cannot identify without further assumptions.
The abstract now leads with selection and states that “whether coverage itself changed valuations depends on when investors first saw the score and on untestable trend assumptions.” The introduction presents the evidence in this order: entry is selective; after entry the gap stops widening but the data do not pin down a causal effect; and a static regression attributes selection to the score. The contributions are restated as evidence of selection, a demonstration of why the valuation effect is hard to identify, and a warning for empirical work. Section 6.2, “What the evidence says about a valuation effect of coverage,” now ends with the reviewer’s point: “With annual data, a provider that chooses whom to score, and recorded scores that may precede publication, a valuation effect of coverage cannot be reliably identified without additional assumptions.” The title already names coverage selection, so we have kept it.
We have rewritten the implications in Section 6.4. For researchers, studies of ESG ratings in the region should model when firms enter coverage, report pre-entry dynamics, and record the download date of their rating data. For rating providers and data users, publishing the date on which each firm’s coverage began, and keeping the vintages of revised scores, would separate what was known from what was filled in later. For exchanges, regulators, and listed firms, the section now states: “Our results do not show that expanding ESG coverage has no valuation benefit; they show that such a benefit cannot be established from these data, so expanding coverage is better justified by the information it provides than by an expected rise in issuer valuations.” The conclusion has been rewritten in the same terms and ends with the data that would move the question forward: archived records of when coverage began, and ownership and trading data.
