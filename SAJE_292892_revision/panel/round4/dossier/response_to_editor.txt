Response to the Editor
Comment E1
“Emphasis on originality and clearer contribution: Please revise the paper in a way that makes its contribution clearer for the reader. You need to motivate your paper in relation to the literature and rewrite wherever possible using simple economic intuitions, including the introduction, to clearly state the purpose of the paper and additional contributions. Please also have a look at past issues of the Southeast Asian Journal of Economics and cite papers that are relevant to your topic.”
We agree and have rewritten the introduction around one question and its economic intuition. The revised manuscript changes Section 1 throughout and adds citations to three papers published in the journal (Sections 2.2, 2.3, 4.3, and 6.3).
The first paragraph now states the purpose in plain terms: an ESG score works as an entry ticket, because funds and indices restricted to scored stocks cannot buy an unscored firm, and the paper asks which firms enter the scored universe and what happens to their valuation around entry. The second paragraph gives the intuition. If a score makes a firm visible to more investors, its price should rise because a wider investor base shares its risk (Merton, 1987); if the score also signals whether the firm is green or brown, prices should move in opposite directions for the two groups. A provider does not score firms at random, however, and if firms enter after a period of rising value, a comparison of scored and unscored firms credits the score with growth that came before it. The paper asks how far the data can separate the effect of being scored from the selection of who is scored. The findings paragraphs now follow this logic, and the contribution paragraph states three contributions: evidence of selection into coverage in Southeast Asian markets, a demonstration of why the valuation effect of coverage is hard to identify, and a practical warning for empirical work in the region.
We searched past issues of the journal for related work and cite three papers where they bear on our argument. Nguyen (2025) shows that ownership structure is associated with the corporate social responsibility scores of Vietnamese listed firms; we cite it in Section 2.3 on which firms disclose enough to be scored. Sudsawasd and Sakdapat (2026) show that local institutional, foreign, and retail investors on the Stock Exchange of Thailand respond differently to trading costs; we cite it in Sections 2.2 and 6.3, where investor participation is the channel through which a score would act and which our data cannot observe. Prukumpai et al. (2022) show that political uncertainty moves Thai stock prices; we cite it in Section 4.3 to motivate the market-level shocks that our country indicators and cluster bootstrap address. We did not find a paper in the journal on ESG rating coverage itself, and we did not add citations that are not related to the argument.
Comment E2
“Abstract: Please write the abstract as clearly as possible, along with the key economic arguments for the ideas you are exploring (you should try to keep it at about 150 words or less). Sometimes when the abstract is hard to comprehend or remains technical, an average reader avoids reading the paper. Thus, it would be in your own interest to make the abstract clear, including the last line as a general forward-looking conclusion on the topic.”
We have rewritten the abstract. It now has 149 words, contains no test statistics or confidence intervals, states the economic argument, and ends with a forward-looking conclusion. The revised abstract reads:
Most listed firms in Southeast Asia have no environmental, social, and governance (ESG) score, so investors who screen on scores cannot hold them. We ask which firms enter the London Stock Exchange Group (LSEG) ESG database and what happens to their valuation around entry. We compare 675 non-financial firms in Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore, and Thailand that received a first recorded score with 2,613 never-scored firms. Entry is selective. Scored firms are larger, and on average their market-to-book ratio had grown 10.0% more than that of similar firms in the years before their first recorded score, a run-up not detected in a broad Malaysian wave. After entry, the valuation gap stops widening, but whether coverage itself changed valuations depends on when investors first saw the score and on untestable trend assumptions. Research and investment screens that compare rated with unrated firms should model who gets rated and when.

We checked every reference for complete details and current publication status. All journal articles have volume, issue, pages, and a DOI where one exists. Two references remain working papers: Berg, Fabisik, and Sautner (2020), on the rewriting of historical Refinitiv ESG scores, and Berg, Heeb, and Kölbel (2022), on the effect of MSCI rating changes on fund holdings and returns. We searched for journal versions of both in October 2026 and found none. We keep them because they are recent, widely cited, and the primary evidence for two points the paper relies on: that the provider we study has revised historical scores, which bears directly on Reviewer A’s first comment, and that ESG fund holdings respond to MSCI ratings. The paper cites no unpublished paper older than these and no paper from a journal we would regard as obscure. The new references are Crump et al. (2009), on estimation under limited overlap, the LSEG (2023) user guide for its point-in-time ESG data, and the three papers from this journal listed under Comment E1. We removed Serafeim and Yoon (2023), which the revised text no longer cites.
